Glider Aerodynamic Design

Design, analysis, and manufacture of a 30 cm wingspan glider optimised for a 10 m/s flight speed under low Reynolds number conditions.

Sep 2025 - Dec 2025 Aerodynamics / CAD / Manufacturing Group project
Project file PRJ-01 Primary tools XFOIL / MATLAB / XFLR5 / SolidWorks Project type Group project My contribution Airfoil analysis, aerodynamic design, CAD, and airframe manufacturing My role Aerodynamics and CAD team member Status Completed Outcome Lightweight glider airframe with thermoformed wing and tail skins

Overview

This project focuses on the design, analysis, and development of a 30 cm wingspan glider optimised for a flight speed of 10 m/s. The objective was to achieve efficient aerodynamic performance under low Reynolds number conditions, typical for small-scale gliders.

The workflow integrated aerodynamic theory, computational tools, CAD development, and practical manufacturing considerations. The final design was taken from airfoil selection through to a lightweight physical airframe and flight testing.

Project Workflow

  1. Define the operating target. Established the 10 m/s flight speed, target mass, wingspan, aspect ratio, and low-Reynolds-number operating environment.
  2. Screen candidate airfoils. Used XFOIL to compare lift coefficient, drag coefficient, lift-curve linearity, drag behaviour, and manufacturability.
  3. Evaluate candidates in MATLAB. Processed the aerodynamic data and compared candidate performance around the intended angle of attack.
  4. Complete the 3D aerodynamic study. Used XFLR5 to assess the full wing, stability, and flight characteristics before committing to the final geometry.
  5. Develop the CAD model. Integrated the selected airfoil into the wing and tail geometry and modelled the fuselage in SolidWorks.
  6. Manufacture the airframe. Laser-cut the airfoil-shaped fuselage formers and wing ribs from 3 mm plywood, then assembled and aligned the structure with super glue.
  7. Form the lifting surfaces. Applied thermoform covering film over the rib structures. Heating and stretching the film produced the required wing and tail skin surfaces while keeping the build lightweight.
  8. Inspect and test. Checked alignment, rigidity, surface finish, and aerodynamic shape before flight testing the completed glider.

Airfoil Selection Methodology

Airfoil selection was based on aerodynamic performance at low Reynolds numbers, where flow behaviour becomes sensitive and conventional airfoils can underperform.

Evaluation criteria

  • Lift coefficient (Cl)
  • Drag coefficient (Cd)
  • Cl versus angle of attack (α) linearity
  • Drag behaviour near operating conditions
  • Manufacturability

Initial screening

AG14 and AG19 were first analysed using XFOIL. Both showed good lift-curve linearity and stable drag characteristics. AG19 produced higher lift but presented manufacturing challenges because of its geometry.

Comparative analysis

Additional low-Reynolds-number airfoils were evaluated: MH32, SD7032, S3021, SD7037, SD7043, SD7080, and SD7084. Performance comparisons at α = 2° highlighted the differences in lift and drag behaviour.

Final selection

SD7032 was selected for its relatively high lift coefficient at low angles of attack, acceptable drag performance, proven low-Reynolds-number effectiveness, and better manufacturability than the AG series.

Analysis and Design Development

  • XFOIL was used for 2D airfoil analysis and Cl-α / Cd-α curve evaluation.
  • MATLAB was used for calculations, data processing, and performance comparison.
  • XFLR5 was used for 3D wing analysis, stability checks, and flight characteristic assessment.
  • SolidWorks was used to model the selected airfoil, wing, tail, fuselage, and final glider assembly.
  • Structural and aerodynamic requirements were balanced to keep the final configuration lightweight and practical to build.

Manufacturing Process

Every airfoil-shaped fuselage former and wing rib was laser-cut from 3 mm plywood. The parts were assembled carefully with super glue, maintaining the correct spacing, alignment, and rigidity throughout the fuselage and lifting-surface structures.

For the wing and tail skins, a thermoform covering film was placed over the rib structures. By heating and stretching the film across the ribs, it adhered to the framework and produced the essential airfoil shape. This method maintained the intended profile while creating a smooth, lightweight skin with a practical workshop process.

Key Takeaways

  • Low-Reynolds-number aerodynamics requires airfoils selected for the actual operating regime.
  • Linear lift behaviour is important for predictable and stable flight.
  • Airfoil selection is a trade-off between aerodynamic performance and manufacturability.
  • Computational tools improve design accuracy when paired with practical testing.
  • Accurate rib alignment and skin tension are critical to preserving the designed airfoil geometry.
