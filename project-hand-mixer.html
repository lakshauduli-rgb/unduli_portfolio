Reverse Engineering - Hand Mixer

A structured reverse-engineering study of a domestic hand mixer housing, combining physical measurement, material identification, CAD reconstruction, and design-for-manufacture analysis.

Jan 2026 - May 2026 Reverse engineering / DFM Group project Completed
Project file PRJ-05 Project type Group project My contribution Reverse-engineered the two-part housing through measurement, ABS identification, CAD reconstruction, and manufacturing analysis My role Reverse engineering and CAD team member Status Completed

Overview

This group project reverse-engineered a domestic hand mixer by studying its two-part housing, internal features, material, assembly strategy, and likely manufacturing process. The housing encloses the motor, motor housing, fan, gears, shaft, and beaters while supporting one-handed use and left- or right-handed operation.

My main contribution was the reverse engineering of the hand-mixer housing. I analysed the top and bottom shells, planned and carried out dimensional and material investigations, reconstructed the geometry in CAD, and connected the observed features to injection moulding and design-for-manufacture decisions.

My Contribution

  • Analysed the housing's external geometry, internal features, surface finish, hardness, flexibility, and opacity.
  • Measured the top and bottom shells using vernier calipers, a micrometer screw gauge, and a height gauge.
  • Investigated shell curvature by sectioning the body and measuring thread lengths placed across each section.
  • Identified the housing material as ABS through float testing, Shore D hardness, density measurement, MatWeb comparison, and burn-test observations.
  • Reconstructed the top shell and bottom shell in SolidWorks and prepared the associated 2D drawings and 3D assembly.
  • Inferred injection moulding, parting-line, ejector-pin, boss, draft-angle, and assembly features from the physical parts.
  • Proposed a handle-ergonomics improvement based on the housing study.

Reverse Engineering Workflow

  1. Inspect the product. Studied the housing's geometry, surface finish, hardness, flexibility, opacity, parting line, bosses, and visible assembly features.
  2. Measure the shells. Used conventional workshop instruments to capture key external and internal dimensions of the separate top and bottom housing shells.
  3. Capture curvature. Sectioned the body and used thread laid across each section to estimate curved profiles where direct measurement was difficult.
  4. Identify the material. Combined float testing, Shore D hardness readings, pycnometer density measurement, MatWeb comparison, and qualitative burn testing.
  5. Reconstruct the geometry. Built separate CAD models and drawings for the top and bottom shells, then assembled them into a complete housing model.
  6. Infer the manufacturing process. Used parting lines, ejector marks, bosses, rounded edges, draft angles, integrated text, and surface finish to identify injection moulding as the most likely process.
  7. Assess design and manufacturing. Considered mould construction, ABS processing, shrinkage, residual stress, directional strength, surface gloss, thread formation, and feasible handle improvements.

Material Identification

Both housing shells were hard, opaque polymers with smooth glossy outer surfaces. They sank in water, indicating a density greater than water. Detailed testing recorded Shore D readings of approximately 78-80 for the top shell and 59-65 for the bottom shell.

Pycnometer measurements recorded densities of approximately 1.068 g/cm³ for the top shell and 1.092 g/cm³ for the bottom shell. Comparison with reference data and burn-test observations supported the conclusion that both shells were made from ABS plastic.

  • Float test: both samples sank in water.
  • Hardness test: separate Shore D readings were taken across both shells.
  • Density test: pycnometer measurements supported the polymer comparison.
  • Burn test: moderate burning, molten dripping, bright yellow/orange flame, pungent smell, black smoke, and continued burning after flame removal.

Housing CAD and Manufacturing Analysis

  • Created separate reconstructed CAD models for the top shell and bottom shell.
  • Produced 2D drawings and a 3D assembly showing how the shells fit together.
  • Identified injection moulding as the most likely housing manufacturing process.
  • Used the visible parting line on the top shell and ejector-pin marks on the bottom shell as process evidence.
  • Recognised bosses, rounded edges, draft angles, and integrated text as features consistent with moulded production parts.
  • Inferred ABS melt temperatures of approximately 200-250 °C and mould temperatures of approximately 50-80 °C from the report's process discussion.
  • Identified formed boss threads and self-tapping screws as the likely assembly method.

Limitations and Improvements

Conventional contact measurements were practical for the project, but optical 3D scanning or a coordinate-measuring machine could capture complex curvature more accurately. The investigation focused on dimensional and material identification; it did not include functional mixer testing, mould fabrication, or a manufactured CAD prototype.

The housing study also informed a handle-ergonomics improvement proposal, linking reverse engineering with an opportunity to improve comfort and usability while preserving the core manufacturing approach.
